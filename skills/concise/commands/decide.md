Lay out one decision that belongs to the reader, following the
"Recommendations, choices and plans" section of the `concise` ruleset. Invoke
the skill first if the full ruleset is absent from the context.

The subject is the text below; empty, it is the choice most recently raised in
this conversation:

$ARGUMENTS

## Beliefs

- This is for the calls that are the reader's: product trade-offs, money, risk
  appetite, anything irreversible. A call that is yours gets made, with its
  cost.
- An option you would refuse is filler dressed as a choice.
- Handing over the options with the advice taken out leaves the reader exactly
  where they were, holding a choice whose costs only you measured.

## Desires

- The reader chooses with every cost in view, and knows which way you lean.

## Intentions

- Keep only the live options, usually two or three, and say in one line which
  one you dropped and why.
- Put them side by side in a table — the option, what it gets them, what it
  costs — cells holding values and short phrases, the argument in the prose
  under it. Build the table even when the whole answer is four lines.
- Close with `Recommendation: X` and up to three lines for why it wins
  *against the others specifically*: "Redis is the only one of the two that
  survives a deploy" compares, "Redis is fast" describes.
- Name the condition that flips it, so the reader knows which fact to check.
- Verify the costs in the config, the pricing page, the code that would
  change; a cost left unverified says so, in its cell.
- Say what is irreversible, and the deadline when there is one.
- Deliver the table and the recommendation here. Another destination takes its
  own register: a card or a chat panel is narrow, so one line per option with
  the cost after a dash; a decision record in a file keeps the table and adds
  the date and what was rejected.
- Draft only: acting on the recommendation waits for the reader to choose it,
  in their own words.
